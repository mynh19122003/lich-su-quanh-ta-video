# Ink Chronometry

A movement about the moment a mark becomes time. A single stroke is treated as a recording: it carries the speed of the hand that made it, the hesitation at its corners, the swell where the pen pressed harder. Every composition makes one gesture its subject, and everything else in the frame measures that gesture.

Space is a quiet, saturated field. It should feel like lacquer or a dyed cloth rather than a flat colour, and it deepens toward the edges as if lit from the centre. The field never competes with the mark. It holds the mark the way a stage holds a single performer: generously, with enough emptiness around it that the gesture reads at a glance and at the size of a thumbnail.

Colour is limited to three voices. The field, one warm metallic tone for the stroke, and one pale tone for the instrument that drew it. There is no decoration outside these three. Each hue is calibrated for its neighbours with painstaking care, so the stroke glows rather than sits on top of the field.

Rhythm comes from measurement. A ring of fine ticks surrounds the gesture, like the frame marks on a film reel or the graduations of a chronometer: short, long, short, counting time around the mark. They are whispered, barely above the field, so they reward close looking without disturbing the silhouette. The stroke itself is shown almost complete, with the instrument still resting at its final point. The work is caught in the instant before it finishes.

Craft is the entire argument. Edges are clean at every size, the stroke tapers the way real ink does, and the instrument is drawn with the precision of a technical illustration. Nothing is approximate. It should look like the result of countless hours of refinement by someone at the top of their field: meticulously crafted, master-level in its restraint, and pristine down to the last pixel. There is no text. The gesture is the word.
